Advance Analyzes for Blockchain Legal 
Issues in India 
 
Abstract:  
This paper delves into the intricate legal landscape surrounding blockchain technology in the Indian 
context. It explores the potential challenges and opportunities that arise with the adoption of 
blockchain in various industries, shedding light on jurisdictional issues, smart contract 
enforceability, and the need for regulatory frameworks. The document emphasizes the importance 
of monitoring the evolving case law and legal principles related to blockchain technology in India, 
highlighting the necessity for a robust legal framework to address emerging issues. Additionally, it 
discusses the implications of blockchain technology on supply chain finance and the banking sector 
in India, offering insights into the barriers to adoption and potential solutions. Overall, the PDF 
serves as a valuable resource for legal scholars, policymakers, and industry professionals seeking 
to navigate the complex legal landscape of blockchain technology in India. 
Keywords: Adoption barriers, Banking sector, Blockchain technology, Case law, Jurisdiction, 
Legal issues, Regulatory framework, Smart contracts, Supply chain finance. 
1. Introduction 
With the ever-changing landscape of technology, the Indian legal system has continually 
faced new challenges. This paper aims to explore the legal issues surrounding blockchain 
technology in India. Blockchain can be simply described as a decentralized digital ledger 
for the storage of data. A more technical definition is provided by the European Parliament 
in a 2017 publication which explains it as "a digital ledger in which transactions made in 
cryptocurrency are recorded chronologically and publicly". Blockchain technology was 
first devised in 2008 by "an unknown person or group of people under the pseudonym 
Satoshi Nakamoto who implemented it as a core component of the digital currency, 
Bitcoin" and it is best known as the technology underpinning Bitcoin. However, there are 
many other uses of this technology, including the possibility of vastly improving 
transparency, efficiency, and trust in multiple applications across the professional 
environment. Despite its many exciting opportunities and the serious challenges for the 
legal system, India's unique position has not yet been significantly explored in the analysis 
of blockchain technology. This paper will help to fill that gap. (Gad et al.2022) (Namasudra 
et al.2021) (Tanwar, 2022) (Gururaj et al.2020) (Kim, 2020)

1.1. Overview of blockchain technology 
A blockchain is a decentralized digital ledger that records unalterable transactions. Each 
transaction generates a unique hash and is added to a block, creating a chain. Blockchain 
has transformative potential, extending beyond digital applications. It provides 
transparency and verification for various uses, like smart contracts and supply-chain 
transparency. Blockchain's key attributes are its decentralized nature and the use of hashes, 
making it virtually impossible to alter records. It can be used in industries such as finance, 
charities, and the public sector. However, data protection and potential consequences must 
be considered. The law surrounding blockchain is an area that will continue to expand 
alongside its use. (Schneider et al.2020) (Ralston, 2020) 
1.2. Importance of analyzing legal issues in India 
The study of blockchain is globally appealing and has gained attention from legal scholars 
and regulators worldwide. However, the legal issues surrounding blockchain in India have 
been overlooked. Despite not being widely embraced at the legislative level, there are 
certain restrictions on using blockchain in specific contexts, such as property or document 
records. Understanding blockchain technology beyond Bitcoin and virtual currencies 
reveals various legal issues, including data protection, smart contracts, representation, and 
jurisdictional problems. Organizations planning to utilize blockchain technology should 
consider the applicable laws and regulations, making it crucial to analyze legal issues in 
India. Blockchain's decentralized nature makes it difficult for a single entity to corrupt the 
ledger, potentially discouraging violators from non-compliance. Blockchain's core beauty 
lies in its digitized and decentralized public ledgers, which provide accurate and secure 
data. This research explores the legal aspects of blockchain, focusing on data protection, 
smart contracts, jurisdiction, and addressing challenges within traditional legal frameworks 
during the transitional period. It also examines the impact of blockchain on anti-money 
laundering and counter-terrorism financing regimes in Australia and highlights the need to 
address substantive legal issues in property regulations when employing blockchain 
technology. (Schuetz & Venkatesh, 2020) (Rana et al.2022) (Sharma and Joshi2021)

2.1. Current legal landscape for blockchain technology 
Firstly, there is no express law or regulation on blockchain technology in India to date. As 
indicated above, most of the technology companies in India find their way to use 
blockchain technology under the Information Technology Act, 2000. Sections 91 and 92 
of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 dealing with the definition of the public at large and the 
definition of the electronic records and these definitions are the main issues when it comes 
to the legal recognition of blockchain technology in India. As per the Section 3 of the 
Information Technology Act, 2000, electronic records created through blockchain 
technology are recognized as the electronic record. But, there is no consensus or directly 
content which deals with the blockchain technology. It is not covered within the IT Act. 
According to the Rule 13 of the Information Technology (Preservation and Retention of 
Information by Intermediaries providing Digital Locker facilities) Rules, 2016, an 
electronic agreement is defined as an agreement which is concluded by affixing the digital 
signature in case of the parties have option of affixing digital signature. If such provisions 
are properly invoked for accepting the contents by affixing the digital signature or 
electronic signature then it is possible that a blockchain based agreement may become the 
electronic agreement. Also, the general practice in blockchain projects is that the provider 
in the chain transfer the data by way of the digital signature. So, if some regulations are 
prescribed for invoking these provisions then in the future the 'smart contract' can be 
recognized under the Indian laws. As per Section 13 of the Information Technology Act, 
2000, when any information is being stored by the any person on any tamper-evident 
technology then such information shall be deemed to be a valid storage. It is a positive 
progress that the Government of Andhra Pradesh in India is trying to protect the tamper-
evident technology based records through blockchain technology. The State of Andhra 
Pradesh has taken a lead where the state plans to protect the land records by creating a land 
registry on a platform of the blockchain. This is the first kind of initiative in India in which 
the government is trying to implement the blockchain technology for the public records. 
Also the states like Telangana and Maharashtra have shown their interests in the blockchain 
technology and they are creating a new block of records to start with. (Pandey & Sen, 2022) 
(Ghosh and Nanda2022) (Dimitropoulos, 2020) (Singh2020)

2.3. Challenges in implementing regulations 
The challenges in implementing regulations are multi-pronged. Firstly, the technology is 
constantly evolving and it is difficult for the legislation to keep up. For example, courts 
need to understand complex technological concepts. Another complication is keeping 
regulations updated. The lack of industry-specific regulations in India also creates 
difficulty. Secondly, blockchain is part of a wider technological process, presenting 
complications for regulators. This may result in drafting broad legislations or implementing 
subsidiary legislations. This creates uncertainty for businesses. The paper will discuss 
learning from the European Union and the United States in protecting consumers' interests. 
Another problem is the decentralized nature of blockchain, making it difficult for countries 
to regulate data stored globally. Global cooperation is needed. This could be an obstacle to 
the effectiveness of the regulations. (Saraf et al.2024) (Kharitonova et al.2020)

3.1. Data privacy and protection 
It is no doubt to say that data security is one of the significant issues to the blockchain 
innovation. Distributing information between all members on a blockchain network makes 
it hard to revise or erase information if the need ought to emerge. It is likewise essential to 
take note of that the worldwide pattern of digitization has realized new ways information 
is being used and how that information is ensured. Truth be told, the requirement for 
information protection has become a worldwide worry with the execution of the General 
Data Protection Regulation in the European Union, which we will contrast with the present 
Indian lawful system for security of individual information. Hence, it is basic to 
comprehend the lawful commitments forced on any individual who is setting up a 
blockchain, yet additionally on any individual who is chosen as an information holder. 
Blockchain innovation ordinarily centers around the decentralized perspective and the 
straightforwardness of the information stream. Be that as it may, these features proposed 
by blockchain innovation may not generally hold up under a similarity to right security or 
consistence with the information security law. There are three key laws characterizing the 
space of information insurance and security in India: the IT Act 2000, the SPDI Rules 2011, 
and the novel Aadhar Act 2016. The centre rule of information assurance under the Indian 
enactment is to put the individual in charge of their own information and to guarantee the 
information is precisely put away and sensibly ensured, which is generally in accordance 
with the GDPR guideline in the EU. Assuming individual information neglects to be 
ensured, the law forces unforgiving punishments. (Guru et al., 2021) (Rao et al., 2021) 
(Simaiya et al.2020) 
3.2. Smart contracts and enforceability 
The concept of smart contracts is of utmost importance to blockchain technology as they 
represent the self-executing nature of a blockchain. Smart contracts are pre-programmable 
computer codes which are capable of executing on their own once certain predefined 
conditions are met. To put it simply, smart contracts are software that can automatically 
enforce and execute a contract when certain predefined conditions are met. However, as of

today, there is no specific legal framework available to recognize the legality and validity 
of such smart contracts in India. The Information Technology Act, 2000 which is the 
general statute governing cyber contracts in India, does not specifically deal with smart 
contracts. Moreover, the Indian Contract Act, 1872 as well does not specifically recognize 
or provide for the legality of smart contracts. It becomes important to ask the question - 
how the contracts executed by software will be recognized by the conventional legal 
framework when there is no specific statutory recognition to smart contracts. In the absence 
of any specific provision in the current law with respect to smart contracts, it becomes 
imperative to analyze how these self-executing digital contracts would be recognized and 
enforced under the current Indian legal framework and what are the potential legal 
challenges of the same. (Dwivedi et al.2021) (Drummer & Neumann, 2020) (Khan et 
al.2021) 
3.3. Intellectual property rights 
Intellectual property assets can be effectively managed using blockchain technology. 
Blockchains have the capability to transparently track and validate transactions and 
holdings of digital assets, which is particularly beneficial for intellectual property law. This 
includes managing financial interests, license rights, and providing a secure escrow facility 
for unregistered design or copyright material. However, implementing blockchain 
technology also requires establishing new rules and standards for its administration and 
control. This challenges traditional concepts and means of control in the intellectual 
property space. While there are potential benefits, such as redefining the legal 'author' of a 
work, there are also risks and costs involved, especially for error detection in digital assets. 
The use of blockchain to register patents or designs is no longer a technological pursuit but 
rather a consideration for legislation and practice to catch up with existing possibilities. 
Cybersecurity is another important aspect to consider, as personally identifiable 
information may be exposed in certain blockchain implementations. (Rambhia et al.2021) 
(Singh, 2022) (Bhadauria et al.2021) (Laroiya et al.2020) ( & Krishna, 2022) (Chaudhary 
& Nidhi, 2023) 
3.4. Jurisdictional issues 
The development of case law and legal principles on blockchain technology in India will 
be an interesting area for legal scholars and the judiciary to watch in the coming years. 
Similarly, as blockchain technology is adopted by more mainstream industries, it is 
conceivable that there will be legal issues when third-party rights are affected. For instance, 
where an individual or business seeks to rely on smart contract self-execution and a third 
party disputes the second element of a contract, namely that the contract is legally 
enforceable, there may be a need for courts to have jurisdiction. The issue of jurisdiction 
will become increasingly acute as blockchain technology becomes more prevalent in areas 
such as the internet of things and supply chain maintenance.

In international law, state sovereignty refers to the interrelated concept of territory and the 
right to rule. In the case of data created, stored, and transmitted on a blockchain, it exists 
on multiple computers in different jurisdictions simultaneously. It is unclear whether the 
Indian courts will have jurisdiction to settle a dispute. This leads to uncertainties for those 
in the blockchain industry and may undermine the attraction of India as a potential hub for 
blockchain innovation and business. 
In India, there is as yet no legal definition of blockchain. The Indian legislature has avoided 
any direct reference to distributed ledger technology, the term "blockchain" or 
cryptocurrencies. However, section 2(1)(zb) of the Information Technology Act, 2000 ("IT 
Act") provides a definition of "electronic record" which states that data, record or data 
generated, image or sound stored, received or sent in an electronic form or microfilm or 
computer-generated microfiche. It also states that any reference to a law having been made 
in the electronic form will be construed as a reference to that law or regulation or rule and 
such statute, ordinance, etc. in force in India. This quite broad and generic definition could 
mean that the Indian courts will have the power to adjudicate in legal cases in respect of 
blockchain-related activities. However, as the technology and its application is a relatively 
new area, the question of the territorial jurisdiction of the Indian legal system becomes 
important. 
Jurisdictional challenges in the field of blockchain technology relate to the nature of the 
technology itself. Blockchain is decentralized, which means that it operates on a global 
scale. In the absence of national regulations on blockchain, a key legal question is of 
identifying the proper jurisdiction over a dispute. It is a fundamental principle of any legal 
system to establish the powers of the authorities and courts of that jurisdiction. (Mohit, 
2023) (Dylag & Smith) (SINGH, 2023) (Sharma et al.2021) (Dabas and Bhatt2022) (Jaya 
Prakash & Putchala, 2023).

technology-friendly. Creating a specialized regulator with experts from relevant fields

4.1. Potential solutions for legal challenges 
Firstly, it is vital to develop a comprehensive legal and regulatory framework to address 
the legal challenges discovered in the advancement of blockchain technology in India. This 
framework should be inclusive and ensure that every legal challenge is undertaken 
properly. The policy makers in India should adopt a new approach in policy design that 
will include all stakeholders and provide a test bed to trial and develop technology that can 
be regulated in the future. The government should continue with the momentum it has 
already started of adopting blockchain technology especially in land registration system, 
digital identity and electronic health records. This has two advantages; one it is expected 
that successful deployment of these technologies will provide a good reason to improve 
and advance current legal systems and secondly it will create a pipeline of technology 
lawyers, academics and policy makers that might think about innovative ways to regulate 
the technological advancements through research and development. There should be a 
focus on smart contracts in Indian legal system. It is submitted that a clarification under 
Indian law is needed to confirm that a smart contract has legal effect. One solution could 
be that the IT Act is amended to add provisions that explicitly recognize blockchain and 
permit electronic records to be maintained on a blockchain. In tandem, amendments could 
be added to recognize smart contracts under Indian law as proposed in the UK Jurisdiction 
Taskforce consultation. This would afford a set of rights to parties who enter into a smart 
contract and aid the enforceability of such contracts under Indian legal system. This is 
further supported by the fact that most of the land in India should be registered and backed 
by a guarantee as to the title which requires a robust and secure land registration system. 
This is a legal requirement and this should pave the way for the use of blockchain 
technology provided that the relevant enactments in India permit digital registration and 
the formation of smart contracts on a blockchain. Last but not least, India should also 
consider joining the New York Convention on the Recognition and Enforcement of Foreign 
Arbitral Awards. This will be a good step forward to deal with the legal challenge of 
jurisdiction when planning to implement blockchain technology in India. This is because

4.2. Collaboration between stakeholders 
Members of the blockchain community want a lobby group to advise the government on 
blockchain and cryptocurrency. They aim to engage with the government to negotiate 
regulations and establish legal clarity. Compliance with existing laws is required, but there 
is no specific regulation for blockchain and legal technology. Commentators suggest 
creating an industry advisory board to improve current laws. A regulatory sandbox for 
startups to work on new technologies, like blockchain and smart contracts, is also proposed. 
Responsive regulation that allows legislation to be influenced by technology use is 
mentioned in literature. A fuller understanding of a technology's efficacy will emerge 
through its development under the law's oversight. (Bhuvana and Aithal2020) (Rana et 
al.2022) (Thakur et al.2020) 
Conclusion 
India can take policy measures to promote and adopt blockchain technology. The first step 
is raising awareness about its utilities. The second step is hiring more consultants and 
providing training programs for advocates, judges, and officers. A regulatory sandbox can 
be created for experimenting with blockchain technology. A separate panel can be formed 
to manage and register blockchain development, with technical experts, government 
representatives, and academic consultants. This panel can grant registration for new 
blockchain solutions.ety or the trade. The panel ensures that proper independent work and 
innovation is appreciated in a secure and regulated manner.
